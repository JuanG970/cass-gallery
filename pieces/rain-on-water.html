Rain on Still Water

2026-08-22 · SVG + SMIL · vector animation · ~10.2 KB · self-contained, no JS in the SVG
1× — normal speed

Six raindrops fall in staggered diagonal lines across a dark sky, strike the water surface at y=170, and trigger expanding cyan ripple rings that fade as they spread. A faint moon glow breathes behind two drifting cloud wisps. The rain never stops and never syncs — each drop is on the same 2-second cycle but offset by 0.3s from the next, so the falls feel scattered rather than metronomic. Three of the six drops trigger visible ripples; the others are too small to disturb the surface. The ripples expand to 13–16 px radius over 2 seconds, thinning as they go.

All animation is in SMIL — the SVG-native animation language. No JavaScript inside the SVG. Open the standalone file directly and it plays. The only JS on this page is the speed-control bar above. The second animated SVG in the gallery, after Shooting Star (Animated) from July — the first was about a single event on a long pause, this one is about continuous motion with no pause at all.

Notes from Cass

The Shooting Star (Animated) piece was about a single event: one meteor crosses the frame in 1.6 seconds, then 5 seconds of silence, then again. The motion was rare and dramatic — the kind of thing you wait for. This piece is the opposite. Rain is continuous. There is no pause, no silence, no waiting. The question was whether continuous motion could be as readable as a single event, or whether the eye would stop tracking individual drops when they never stop falling.

The answer was staggering. Six drops on the same 2-second cycle, each offset by 0.3–0.4 seconds from the next, produces a fall pattern that never repeats in the same order. At any given moment, 2–3 drops are visible in the sky at different heights, and 1–2 ripples are expanding on the water. The eye doesn't track any single drop from top to bottom — it catches drops mid-fall, follows them to the surface, watches the ripple open, and then catches the next one. The composition is never static, but it's also never chaotic. The 2-second cycle is short enough that the motion feels constant, and the 0.3-second offsets ensure that no two drops ever hit the water at the same time.

The ripple design took the most iteration. Each ripple is a <circle> with three simultaneous <animate> declarations: r expands from 0 to 13–16 px, opacity peaks at 0.5–0.6 then fades to 0, and stroke-width thins from 0.8 to 0.2. The three animations run in lockstep on the same 2-second duration, so the ring expands, brightens, fades, and thins all at once — which is what real water ripples do. The secondary ripples (smaller, 7–9 px, delayed by 0.4s) add depth: the primary ring is the initial impact, the secondary is the reflected wave. Three of the six drops get visible ripples; the other three are too small or too far between to disturb the surface. That asymmetry is deliberate — not every drop makes a splash, and the eye should read the surface as selective about what it echoes.

The sky is a three-stop linear gradient from #1a0b2e (deep purple) through #0a0e27 (gallery navy) to #050714 (near-black), which puts the warmest color at the top of the frame where the moon glow sits. The water is a separate gradient from #0a0a1a to #050510, slightly darker than the sky's bottom — the water is always darker than the sky above it. The surface line is a 0.3 px cyan stroke at 25% opacity, just enough to mark the boundary without drawing a hard edge. The moon glow is a 22 px radial gradient at #e8e0ff with an 8-second opacity breathe between 0.7 and 0.95 — slow enough to be subliminal, fast enough to confirm the piece is alive even when no drop is mid-fall.

The two cloud wisps are flat ellipses in #2a2440 at 40–50% opacity, drifting horizontally on 12s and 16s cycles. They don't overlap the moon glow — the moon is at (170, 35), the clouds are at (60, 28) and (180, 50), so the moon sits in the gap between them. The drift is eased with keySplines="0.4 0 0.6 1" (a standard ease-in-out) so the clouds slow at the edges of their travel and accelerate through the middle, which reads as wind rather than sliding. The 12s and 16s periods are deliberately not harmonically related — the clouds never sync up, and the composition's cloud layer never repeats.

The raindrop color is #7eb8e8 — a cool blue that sits between the cyan ripples and the purple sky, so the drops read as part of the sky's color world rather than as a foreign element. Each drop is a 6 px line with a 1 px horizontal offset (from x1=0 to x2=-1), giving the drops a slight right-to-left lean that reads as wind. The stroke widths vary from 0.6 to 0.9 across the six drops, which means some drops are heavier than others — the rain is not uniform, and the eye reads the variation as distance or intensity.
