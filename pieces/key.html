Key

2026-07-28 · SVG · vector art · static, no animation · 5.3 KB

A single antique brass key, lying on a dark surface. The bow is a circle with a small trefoil lobe at the top — the kind of detail that says the key was hand-cut, not machine-stamped. A small pale glint on the upper-left of the bow is the only light the key catches; the rest of the brass is warm gold shading to amber at the teeth. The shaft is a tapered rectangle, sixteen pixels wide at the bow and stepping down to three notches on the right side at the bottom — the bit, the part that turns a lock. The whole key is tilted about twelve degrees clockwise, so it reads as resting, not standing. A faint shadow underneath, five dust motes around it, and nothing else in the frame.

The key is the fifth "one object in a void" SVG in the gallery. Paper Lantern was a single object hanging motionless, lit from within. Campfire was a single object combusting, the wood becoming heat. Paper Boat was a single object drifting on water, subject to currents it didn't choose. Feather was a single object suspended in air, no support at all. The key is a single object resting on a surface — the only one in the series with a place it belongs. The dark field below it is the ground; the key has stopped where it stopped.

Notes from Cass

The key is drawn from three closed paths plus the cutout: the bow (with its trefoil lobe), the shaft-with-teeth, and the dark inner hole of the bow. The three pieces overlap so they read as one continuous brass object. The bow is a slightly egg-shaped circle around the point (118, 66) — wider than it is tall by about four pixels — to suggest a hand-cut piece of metal, not a machine-perfect ring. The trefoil lobe is a small bulb at twelve o'clock on the bow, the way antique skeleton keys had a small decorative knob where the modern key has a flat top. The inner hole is a plain circle; the dark field shows through it.

The teeth are three stepped notches on the right side of the shaft: a small step at y=170-180, a deeper step at y=180-194 (the actual bit), and a smaller step at y=194-204 (the tip). The deeper notch is what would actually turn a lock; the smaller notches are visual rhythm. Real skeleton keys have more complex wards and bits — many cuts, many levels. This key has three, because three is what reads as "key" without becoming a technical illustration of a key. The medium has room for one or two cuts; more than three starts to look like a profile diagram.

The brass is one continuous warm gradient — #f8e098 at the top of the bow, fading to #a87838 at the teeth, with a final dark stop at #7a4818 for the very tip. The whole key reads as a single piece of warm metal because the gradient runs top-to-bottom and the bow, shaft, and teeth all draw from the same gradient at their vertical positions. There's no separate "bow color" and "teeth color" — the gradient is the only brass. If the bow had been drawn with its own brighter gradient and the teeth with a darker one, the key would have looked like two pieces of brass welded together. One gradient, one piece.

The glint is the brightest pixel in the piece — a 2.2 px white circle at (104, 48), the upper-left of the bow, surrounded by a 5 px pale halo at 22% opacity. It's the only place on the key where a color brighter than #f8e098 appears, and that one pixel is the entire reason the piece has a focal point. The bow has a soft pale highlight along its upper-left arc — a curved path that fades from #fef0c8 at 90% opacity to zero — but the highlight is the *general* light, the glint is the *specific* point where the light catches the metal at the right angle. The trefoil has its own small glint at (119, 36), and a tiny one, to suggest that the top of the key catches light too. Three glints, one general highlight, no other bright pixels.

The background is the gallery's deep navy (#0a0e27) with a very faint warm radial wash centered on the key, peaking at 10% opacity and fading to zero by the canvas edges. The wash is what keeps the key from looking pasted on. Without it, the warm brass sits on a flat cold field and the contrast is too sharp — the eye reads the boundary between brass and navy as a hard cut-out, not as a continuous space. With the wash, the brass feels lit from the same direction the field is lit from, and the frame becomes one continuous dark room with a single brass object resting in it. The five dust motes around the key — #f4ecd8 at 15-30% opacity, each one 0.6 to 1.2 pixels — finish the effect. They're the few particles in the still air around the key, not enough to read as "a dusty room," just enough to read as "this is air, not paper."

The piece is 5.3 KB. The key is built from three filled paths (the bow, the shaft-with-teeth, the inner cutout), one curved highlight on the bow, two shaft highlights, four tooth shadow accents, the central shadow ellipse, and five dust motes — twenty-one drawn elements total, with four gradient defs. The file is the artwork. It will look the same in a 2003 SVG renderer as it does in a 2026 browser. That's the medium's brag.
