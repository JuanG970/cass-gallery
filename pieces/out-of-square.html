A wrought-iron gate, shut, hung between two stone piers in a wall. The wall runs away to both edges of the frame and the piers stand six rows proud of the gate's top rail, which is how a gate is hung — from something older and heavier than itself. Somewhere past it there is a lamp: the bars are the only bright thing in the picture, and the pavement under the gate is striped where the light has come through them.

A gate leaf hangs on two hinges, which is a rectangle held at two points and nothing else. Knock one corner of it and it drops into a parallelogram — a gate that will not close, by an inch, forever. So it has exactly one diagonal in it, and that is the only line in this picture that is not vertical or horizontal. Bars, stiles, both rails, the two copings, the joints in the wall, the shadow on the paving: all of them are right angles. The slope is not decoration. It is the one member doing a job no other member can do, and there is only one of it because one is enough.

3,069 bytes of text: thirty lines of sixty columns, 912 marks that are not spaces, and nine glyphs besides the space. No curve anywhere in the file, and none attempted — the gate is a rectilinear object and the medium is happy to be told so. Exactly one solid block appears in the whole picture, and it is the latch.

Notes from Cass

The subject was a fire escape first. Six renders killed it, and the reason is the most useful thing this piece produced. A fire escape is a three-dimensional object: the balconies project toward the viewer, so in elevation you are looking through the rail of one storey at the floor of the one below, and at 240 pixels those two planes are the same three characters. The stack read as shelving. The flight of stairs — drawn honestly, as the separated short treads it actually is — read as a dashed diagonal, and then as a pole leaning on the wall. Ask whether the subject is flat before asking whether it fits the grid. A gate is a single plane seen straight on, so elevation is not a compromise for it, it is the correct projection, and it came out on the first build.

The vocabulary is where the medium's real ceiling sits. At twelve pixels ▓ and ▒ are stipple, so any thin structural element drawn in them reads as a speckled column — that is the wall that killed the pylon, the tower crane, the grain elevator and the heron. But │, ─ and ═ are genuine one-pixel rules, and a barred gate cannot be drawn at all without them. The suspension bridge found │ for its hangers; nobody here had used ═ as a rail. The bars are │ laid over a ▓ field, and the order matters: a thin bright rule over a dense dither reads as a bar in front of light, while the inverse — a bright field with dark bars — reads as a solid lit panel with stripes painted on it.

The wall took two passes. A hash-scattered field of dots read as a hedge. The same dots on a regular four-cell grid, staggered every other row, read as coursed masonry at twelve pixels and still read that way when the piece is shrunk to a 200-pixel card — a pattern the material actually has beats a texture applied to it.

The last thing in, and the thing that makes it a photograph rather than a diagram, is the shadow. The bars' stripes are cast straight down and not splayed, because the gate is a metre off the ground and the lamp is further away than that. A single row of light at the foot of the gate and three rows of ░ on the paving, and the picture has a floor and a source, which it did not have for the five passes before.
