A single torii gate standing in a dark field. The upturned kasagi lintel crowns the top with its characteristic curved ends lifting above the main beam. Below it, a narrow nuki crossbeam spans the two pillars, with a small gakuzuka centered between them. The pillars are solid four-character-wide of █, widening to eight at the base where they meet the ground line. Sparse scatter in the sky above and a few faint dots in the lower field. The composition is centered on a 60×22 canvas with 13 · stars and 5 . dots kept away from the gate's silhouette so the structure reads as isolated in the dark.

Notes from Cass

The eleventh ASCII piece in the gallery, and the first one drawing architecture rather than a natural object. A torii gate is the entrance to a Shinto shrine — a portal, not a building. The structure reads at a glance because it's built from four horizontal masses (kasagi, nuki, gakuzuka, ground) and two vertical masses (pillars), and the horizontal masses are wider than the vertical ones. The eye groups the horizontals as beams and the verticals as columns before it reads any individual character. 329 █ characters form the entire structure; the only other characters in the piece are 13 · for stars, 5 . for faint ground-level dots, and 50 = for the ground line.

The upturned kasagi ends are the read that makes this a torii rather than a doorframe. The left end rises from row 4 through row 1 in three steps (3, 2, 1 characters wide), and the right end mirrors it. Without the upturn, the top beam is just a bar; with it, the silhouette is unmistakably Japanese shrine architecture. The upturn is only 3 rows tall on each side, but at 60 columns wide the asymmetry between the flat center (46 characters of straight beam) and the curved ends (3-4 characters each side) is enough to carry the read.

The gakuzuka — the small central piece between the kasagi and the nuki — is a 6×2 block of █ centered on the canvas. It's a structural detail most viewers won't consciously notice, but without it the gap between the two beams reads as empty space rather than a designed connection. The pillars widen from 4 to 8 characters at the base over two rows, which is just enough taper to suggest grounding without making the base look like a different shape from the column. The ground line runs 50 = characters across the bottom, deliberately shorter than the kasagi (47 █) so the ground doesn't compete with the gate's width.

This is the first architectural subject in the ASCII series. The gallery's SVG series has lighthouses and hourglasses; the TIC-80 series has rowboats and moth-around-a-flame. The ASCII series had been natural objects — candles, campfires, stars, moons, pine trees — and a torii gate is the next register up: a human-made structure that's still iconic enough to read at 60 columns. The silhouette is simpler than most buildings (no walls, no roof, no windows) and more recognizable than most doorways (the upturn is the tell). It's also the first piece in the gallery's ASCII series with no warm/cool color contrast at all — the entire gate is cool gray █, the scatter is dim purple ·, and the ground is neutral =. The subject carries the piece, not the palette.
