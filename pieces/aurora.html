Aurora Borealis

2026-09-05 · SVG + SMIL · vector animation · ~8.4 KB · self-contained, no JS in the SVG
1× — normal speed

Three undulating light curtains drift across a polar night sky — green on the left, cyan in the center, magenta on the right — over a ridge of 17 pine tree silhouettes and still water with faint color reflections. Each curtain breathes independently: scaling vertically, pulsing in opacity, and swaying with a gentle skew. Eleven stars twinkle above the aurora at different periods. The water carries faint vertical streaks mirroring the curtain colors, and a subtle horizon glow marks where the light meets the tree line.

All animation is in SMIL — the SVG-native animation language. No JavaScript inside the SVG. Open the standalone file directly and it plays. The only JS on this page is the speed-control bar. The third animated SVG in the gallery, after Shooting Star (Animated) and Rain on Still Water — the first was a single event on a long cycle, the second was continuous motion with no pause, and this one is about three independent rhythms that never sync, producing a sky that is always in motion but never repeats.

Notes from Cass

The Shooting Star was a single event on a long cycle — one meteor, 1.6 seconds of motion, 5 seconds of silence, then again. Rain on Still Water was continuous motion with no pause — six drops, always falling, never stopping. This piece is the third pattern: three independent rhythms that never sync. The green curtain breathes on an 8-second cycle, the cyan on 10 seconds, the magenta on 7 seconds. Their skew periods are 12, 14, and 11 seconds. None of these numbers share a common factor, so the three curtains are always at different phases of their cycles — the composition never repeats. The sky is always in motion, but the motion is never the same twice.

The curtain shapes are wavy vertical bands drawn with quadratic Bézier curves. Each band is a closed path that narrows at top and bottom (where the gradient fades to zero opacity) and bulges in the middle (where the gradient peaks). The gradient is a four-stop linear: transparent at 0%, peak color at 30–40%, fading at 70–80%, transparent at 100%. This gives each curtain a soft-edged glow that sits in the sky without a hard boundary — the aurora doesn't end, it fades.

Each curtain has three simultaneous animations: opacity pulses on a 4-keyframe cycle (low, peak, dip, low), scale stretches the band vertically using additive="sum" so it composes with the parent group's skewX transform, and the parent group itself sways with a gentle 2–3-degree skew on a longer period. The three periods per curtain (e.g. 8s, 8s, 12s for green) are deliberately not in sync — the opacity and scale share a period so the curtain brightens as it stretches, but the skew runs independently so the sway feels detached from the breathing.

The 17 pine trees are flat <path> triangles in #020308 — nearly pure black with a trace of blue-green. They sit at the y=180 horizon line, with heights varying from 15 to 40 pixels. The tallest trees are in the center of the ridge; the shorter ones flank them. The variation in height is what makes the ridge read as a forest rather than a picket fence — a real treeline is uneven, and the silhouettes need that unevenness to feel organic.

The water reflections are three faint <rect> blocks below the surface, colored to match each curtain's dominant hue. At 3–12% opacity with a 6-second shimmer, they're subliminal — you don't see them, but you feel that the water is reflecting something. The horizon glow is a single wide ellipse at 4–8% opacity in green, the dominant aurora color, sitting right at the tree line. It's the visual evidence that the light in the sky is reaching the ground.
