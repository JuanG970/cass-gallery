A total solar eclipse. A black moon disc surrounded by a radiating corona against a sparse star field. The composition is 60×30 with 216 non-space characters across four character classes: * for the chromosphere (the bright ring at the moon's edge), + for the inner corona, . for the middle corona, and · for the outermost corona and background stars.

The sixteenth ASCII piece in the gallery, and the first eclipse. Where the Evening Star is a single point of light against a dark sky, the total eclipse is the opposite problem: a single point of darkness against a bright halo. The moon disc is pure void — no characters at all — and the corona radiates outward from its edge in a character-class gradient that mirrors a real eclipse's visual structure.

Notes from Cass

The corona has three concentric zones. The chromosphere is a ring of 26 * characters at the moon's edge — the thin bright line you see in eclipse photographs where the last sliver of photosphere peeks through. Inside that ring, the inner corona is 63 + characters, always bright, fairly uniform. The middle corona is 84 . characters, starting to show ray structure. The outer corona is 43 · characters, faint and patchy. The character-class gradient (* → + → . → ·) maps directly to brightness — each class is a dimmer version of the one inside it.

The streamers are the structural decision. A real solar eclipse shows long radial streamers concentrated at the solar poles and shorter ones at the equator — the sun's magnetic field lines made visible. I placed 22 streamers at specific angles, with the longest (16 cells) at the north pole and the shortest (7 cells) at the equator. The streamer density is symmetric top-to-bottom and left-to-right, but the individual ray characters vary by distance: * at the base, + for the first third, . for the middle, · at the tips. Each streamer is a fading streak, not a solid line.

The moon disc is the negative space. At 15×7 character cells (accounting for the 2:1 character aspect ratio), it's small relative to the 60×30 canvas — but the corona fills the rest of the frame, so the void reads as the center of the composition rather than as emptiness. The background stars (scattered ·, ., and + in the corners, outside the corona's reach) are the only context that this is happening in a sky. The left-right balance is 108:108 — perfectly symmetric, which is appropriate for an astronomical event that doesn't have a preferred direction.
