Dwell Canvas

2026-11-20 · Canvas · 960×540 · one pen, four pendulums, five minutes of drawing · 11.6 KB of JS, no library

no interaction · the pendulums die away, the pen lifts, a fresh card goes down

A harmonograph is a pen hung from two pendulums. The pen's position is the sum of four decaying sine waves — two of them swinging it sideways, two swinging it up and down — so the mark it leaves is a Lissajous figure that never quite closes, because the frequency ratios are near-rational rather than rational. Instead of retracing one line a hundred times, the pen lays a slightly different line on every pass, and the drawing creeps into a lace.

What makes the picture is the ink rule, and it is the only rule in the piece. The alpha of each tiny segment is proportional to dt / len — the time the pen spent on it — not to how far it travelled. So the drawing is not shaded. It is a record of speed. Every dark place on that card is a place the pen was slow: the turning point of each swing, where it hangs for a moment before coming back, and the whole middle of the sheet, because the amplitudes decay at e−t/105 and after five minutes the swing is down to about six per cent of where it started.

Which is why the outside of the figure is faint and the inside is almost solid, and why that gradient is not a gradient at all. The pen is fastest when the pendulums are at full amplitude and slowest when they are nearly stopped, and the ink follows the speed. A camera and a pendulum are the same instrument pointed in opposite directions — one records how long the light fell, the other how long the pen rested — and this is what a hundred and fifty years of pendulum drawings have always been: photographs of time, made the hard way.

Each card takes five minutes of the machine's time, run at five times speed so you can watch it in one. Then the pen lifts, the card holds for a few seconds, and the next one goes down, with different detuning and a different starting phase. No two are alike; the reason is a percent or two of frequency.

Notes from Cass

The whole piece is two ideas and both of them are about not drawing what you mean. I did not set out to draw a lacy figure; I set out to record a pen. The lace is what a pen does when you let it swing for a hundred and fifty cycles at ratios that are wrong by one per cent.

The first version failed for a reason worth writing down: I ran the machine in real time and it produced almost nothing. At a sixth of a hertz and a fifty-second session you get seven or eight cycles, and a detune of three-quarters of a per cent is a phase drift of a twentieth of a cycle — the pen simply retraced its own line, and the result was one thin loop with a couple of ghosts. A harmonograph needs hundreds of cycles for the passes to spread out into a lace. That is why the clock is wrong on purpose: five machine minutes per card. The compression is not a compromise, it is the piece. A real harmonograph takes quarter of an hour and nobody watches it; this one takes a minute and you do.

The second thing is the line itself. My first pass drew segments at a fixed timestep, which meant the segments got shorter as the pendulums died, and short segments with a fixed alpha turned the middle of the card into a black disc. Driving the pen at a fixed length instead — 1.8 pixels, which is below the eye's threshold at any speed — and computing the ink from time per length is what makes the darkness mean something rather than accumulate accidentally.

It runs at a locked 60 fps at 960×540. The paper, the table, the grain and both shadow passes are built once at load into offscreen canvases; the only per-frame work is the stroke and three blits.
