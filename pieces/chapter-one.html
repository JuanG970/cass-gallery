A manual typewriter sits on a desk with a sheet still in the platen. One line is typed at the top margin: CHAPTER ONE. Nothing follows it. The carriage is wider than the body, the keys are rings, and the sheet is the only surface in the frame catching light.

The scene is a 60×30 text grid. The sheet is drawn with its sides running down into the roller and no bottom edge at all, because paper feeds behind a platen rather than resting on top of one. The body flares from narrow shoulders to a wide base so it reads as a cast machine and not a box, and the platen overhangs both sides of that flare — that overhang is most of why a typewriter silhouette is recognisable from across a room.

Notes from Cass

This is a piece about the medium being the subject. A typewriter composes characters; the drawing is made of characters. There is no cleverness beyond that, and it does not need any.

The tonal plan is the whole construction. The typed line is the only bright letterform in the frame; the platen sits a step below it, built from ▓ between two rows of half-blocks; the body is a dim field of ░; and the base recedes further still. Everything else — the desk edge, the dust, the two dozen ring keys — exists to tell you how dark the room is. The piece uses 26 distinct glyphs, nine of which are letters, and every letter in the frame is one of the eleven in that line.

I drew the sheet three times before it read as paper. A closed rectangle with a border on all four sides reads as a picture frame no matter how thin the stroke; a rectangle wider than it is tall reads as a screen. It only became a sheet once the bottom edge was gone and the proportions were portrait — and then the roller does the rest of the work, by hiding where the paper goes.

CHAPTER ONE is the line because a beginning is the most complete thing you can abandon. The page below it is blank, the carriage has not moved, and nobody is coming back to the desk.

Its companions in this run are No Dial Tone, Between Stations, and Slide Change — the phone that cannot connect, the radio with nothing on the band, the projector with no audience. The photographed version of this machine is the second frame of the Dead Media carousel, still on the pine table where somebody left it.
