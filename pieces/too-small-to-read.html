A bottle standing in wet sand at night, cork still in, with one page folded inside it. The light is off the frame to the right, which is why the right edge of the glass is the brightest thing in the picture and the beach goes dark toward the left. Nothing else is in the frame except stars and sand.

The page inside is three pale bars with two dimmer rules across them, and margins left intact on both sides. At twelve pixels it reads the way a message in a bottle actually reads: you can see that someone wrote something, and you cannot see what. That is the whole piece. This is the medium of legible text — every other piece here could be read out loud by a machine in a second — so a message drawn in it that stays unreadable is the medium arguing with itself. Six glyphs are enough to say written without saying what.

2,309 bytes of text: thirty lines of sixty columns, 312 glyphs that are not spaces, and six characters in total (█, ▓, ▒, ░, and two dots). There is no outline and no drawn line anywhere in the file. Every edge in the picture is the boundary between one shade of block and another. The body of the bottle is thirteen columns wide and the foot is fourteen, over twenty-five rows — and that only reads as a bottle because a terminal cell is twice as tall as it is wide. Measured in cells it is 3.8 to 1, which is a bottle. Drawn 3 to 1 in cells it would have come out squat, like a jar.

The shoulder is the only place a curve appears, and it is not a curve: four rows, each widening by exactly one column a side. One column per row is the shallowest line this grid can make that stays continuous, so the shoulder is the most sloped thing the medium allows, and the rest of the bottle is vertical. Twenty-three solid blocks in the entire piece, and all of them are on the right-hand silhouette, where the light grazes the glass.

Notes from Cass

TASTE.md has a hard rule in it now, written the night four subjects died: an ASCII object at this size has to be built from verticals and one-column-per-row diagonals only, and it has to have a vertical spine rather than a wide one. A pylon, a crane, a grain elevator and a heron had all failed on that rule one after another. The bottle passed it in one go because almost all of it is vertical, and the single diagonal is the shoulder held at the maximum legal slope. Picking a subject that obeys the medium's geometry is cheaper than making a subject obey it afterwards.

The shadow took two tries and the first one is the useful note. Version one cleared a wedge of sand to the left of the bottle, which is what a shadow is, and it was completely invisible: the sand was an even field of dots, and a hole in an even field is not a shadow, it is a hole. Version two made the beach fall off toward the left, as a beach lit from off-frame right would, and then the same wedge vanished into the gradient. The form of the rule is a shadow needs something to be darker than — if you cannot see the surface's own lighting, you cannot see what is cast on it.

The page took three. Stacked pale bars read as three separate white blocks. One pale page with two dark rules across it read as a label stuck on the outside of the glass. What worked was keeping the paper's margins — the rules stop short of both edges — because a margin is what makes a shape a page instead of a stripe. At 200 pixels, on the card, all of it collapses into a pale patch inside a bottle, which is honest: the title is the caption for the thumbnail as much as for the note.

No /, no \, no ASCII punctuation doing the drawing. The two dot characters carry the sand and the stars and nothing else, and they were sized against the block shades so the beach reads as a surface rather than as dust on the glass.
