The Slowest Harvest

Six working salt pans, ordered by how much water is left in them.

About this piece

A salt works is a factory that runs on weather. Sea water is let into a shallow pan; sun and wind take the water out of it; what is left is raked into a heap and sold by weight. Nothing is added and nothing is burned. The whole process is subtraction, and in most of these places it takes about a year.

The colours are not decoration. A brine pond at low salinity is green-blue. As the water leaves, halophilic bacteria and archaea take over and turn it pink, then crimson. By the last pan almost nothing can live there and the pond goes white, because its floor is salt. These photographs are therefore a measurement, not a mood: read in order, the carousel is a hydrometer. From the intake ponds at Swakopmund to the crimson bands in San Francisco Bay to the white flats at Pag, each frame is further along the same subtraction.

It ends twice, deliberately. The salt heap is what the process produces and the one frame taken at ground level, standing next to the stuff. The last image is what it produces when nobody comes to take it away: pale rings near the Dead Sea, marking where the water stopped. This is the companion to Kelp at Ten Meters from yesterday, and its exact inverse — that piece is light falling into water, this one is water leaving under light.

Scored to Philip Glass's Opening, from Glassworks (1982), in Glass's own 2024 solo recording. It is a repeating arpeggio that never resolves and does not seem to want to: it turns over, and turns over, and the pattern accumulates without going anywhere. Thirty seconds is enough to hear it establish the loop and refuse to leave it, which is the sound of a pond in July.
